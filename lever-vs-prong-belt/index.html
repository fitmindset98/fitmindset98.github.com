Lever vs Prong Belt: Which Should You Buy?

Lever and single-prong lifting belts compared on speed, tightness, adjustability and price, plus who each one suits.

Short answer

Both give the same support once they're on. A lever belt is faster and easier to get very tight, but it needs a screwdriver to adjust. A single-prong belt adjusts instantly and costs less, which makes it the better first belt for most lifters.

Both lever and prong belts do the same job once they're on: give you a stiff, uniform wall to brace against. The difference is entirely about how they close, how easily you can adjust them, and what that means across a training session.

Lever belts in a nutshell

A lever belt closes with a metal lever that snaps shut. You set the tightness once by screwing the lever into a pair of holes, and after that, it's one flick on and one flick off.

  • Pros: very fast, very tight, consistent fit every time, easy to release between sets.
  • Cons: changing the setting needs a screwdriver, usually costs more, and doesn't handle body-weight changes well.

Prong belts in a nutshell

A prong belt works like a regular trouser belt, with a single prong (or two) that goes through a hole. Single-prong is the one to get. Double-prong belts take longer to fasten and don't add any support.

  • Pros: adjust in seconds, cheaper, easy to share or use for different lifts at different settings.
  • Cons: harder to get really tight, slower to take on and off, and a stiff new belt can take some wrestling.

Which one should you pick?

Choose a lever belt if you train the same lifts at the same body weight most of the year, you value speed between sets, and you want it as tight as possible for heavy squats.

Choose a single-prong belt if you're buying your first belt, you're bulking or cutting, you want different tightness for squats and deadlifts, or you share equipment at a home gym.

For most beginners and intermediates, single prong is the safer bet. You can always move to a lever later, once you know your preferred fit. If you're still figuring out the right fit, our guide on how tight a lifting belt should be walks through it.

What about Velcro and nylon belts?

Velcro nylon belts are comfortable and good for Olympic lifting, CrossFit-style training and higher-rep work, where you need to move freely. For heavy, slow squats and deadlifts, they usually can't match the stiffness of a leather belt. If your goal is maximal strength, think of them as a second belt, not your main one.

Features that matter more than the buckle

  • Uniform width. A belt that's the same width all the way around (usually 4 inches, or 10cm) gives you support at the front, where you brace hardest.
  • Thickness. 10mm suits most people. We compare it with 13mm in 10mm vs 13mm belts.
  • Quality leather and stitching. A cheap belt that folds or cracks within months is no bargain.
  • Correct sizing. Measure around your waist where the belt will sit, not your trouser size.

Questions lifters ask

Is a lever belt better than a prong belt?

Not in terms of support. Lever belts are faster and easier to tighten, while prong belts are more adjustable. Pick based on how you train.

Is a double-prong belt better than a single prong?

No. Double-prong belts take longer to fasten and don't add support. Single prong is the better choice.

Can I adjust a lever belt?

Yes, but you have to unscrew the lever and move it to different holes, which takes a screwdriver and a few minutes.
