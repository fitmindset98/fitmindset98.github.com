At What Weight Should You Start Using a Lifting Belt?

Practical guidelines for when to start wearing a lifting belt, based on relative effort, rough strength benchmarks and bracing skill.

Short answer

There's no fixed number. Most lifters use a belt for sets around 80% of their one-rep max and above, once they can brace well without it. As a rough guide, that's often when squats reach about 1.25 to 1.5 times body weight and deadlifts about 1.5 times.

"What weight should I start using a belt at?" is one of the most common beginner questions, and the honest answer is that there's no magic number. There are, however, a few reliable guidelines that will get you close enough.

Think percentages, not plates

A 100kg squat is heavy for one person and a warm-up for another. A more useful way to think about it is relative effort. Most lifters who use a belt put it on for sets at roughly 80% of their one-rep max and above, or for any set that feels like a real grind.

Don't know your max? a one-rep max calculator gives you a solid estimate from a recent hard set, so you never have to test a true max.

Rough body-weight guidelines

These aren't research-based cutoffs, just practical rules of thumb that many coaches use. A belt starts to make sense once your lifts are in this range for sets of a few reps:

LiftBelt starts to make sense around
Back squatAbout 1.25 to 1.5 times body weight
DeadliftAbout 1.5 times body weight
Overhead pressAbout 0.6 to 0.75 times body weight

If you're below these numbers, you'll almost always get more out of practicing technique and bracing than from adding a belt.

Skill comes before the belt

Strength numbers are only half the picture. Before you wear a belt, you should be able to:

  • Take a full breath into your belly and sides, not just your chest.
  • Hold that brace through the whole rep, without letting the air out at the hardest point.
  • Keep your heavy sets looking like your warm-ups.

If any of these are shaky, work on them beltless first. Our guide on how to brace with a lifting belt includes drills you can practice without one.

How to use a belt across a workout

A simple structure that works for most programs:

  1. Warm-ups: no belt. Use them to rehearse your brace.
  2. Lighter working sets (below about 75 to 80% of max): mostly beltless.
  3. Top sets and heavy triples, doubles and singles: belt on.
  4. Back-off sets: your choice. Going beltless here keeps your trunk strength honest.

That way you get the belt's benefit on your hardest sets while still training your midsection to do its job without it.

What about starting weights for beginners?

If you're still working out how much to load in your first months, FitBesidesHealth's guide on how much weight beginners should start with is a better place to start than any belt. Those first months are when strength climbs fastest, and nothing you can strap on will speed that up.

Signs you've waited long enough

  • Your trunk, not your legs, is what gives out on heavy squats and pulls.
  • You've trained consistently for at least six months.
  • Your program now includes regular heavy sets of 1 to 5 reps.
  • You want to compete, where a belt is standard equipment.

Ready to pick one? Start with whether you need a belt at all, then decide between lever and prong.

Questions lifters ask

Should beginners use a lifting belt?

Usually not in the first several months. That's the best time to learn to brace without one.

Should I wear a belt for warm-ups?

No. Use warm-ups to practice bracing without the belt, and put it on for your heaviest sets.

Do I need a belt for overhead press?

Not usually, but many lifters wear one for heavy sets of overhead press once the weights get challenging.
