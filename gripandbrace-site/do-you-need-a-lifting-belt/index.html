Do You Need a Lifting Belt? An Honest Answer for Beginners

What a lifting belt really does, who benefits, who can skip it, and the signs you're ready for one.

Short answer

Not in your first months of lifting. A belt helps once your squats and deadlifts are heavy relative to your body weight and you can already brace well without one. It amplifies a good brace. It doesn't replace one or fix bad form.

Walk into any serious gym and you'll see belts everywhere, often on people doing lat pulldowns. So it's fair to wonder if you're missing something by training without one. Here is the honest version.

What a lifting belt actually does

A belt doesn't hold your spine in place. It gives your abdominal wall something firm to push against. When you take a big breath and brace into the belt, pressure inside your trunk (intra-abdominal pressure) rises, and your torso becomes stiffer under load.

That effect has been measured for decades. Early work by Harman and colleagues in 1989 found that wearing a belt increased intra-abdominal pressure during heavy deadlifts, and later studies on squats have reported similar results. You can browse the research yourself on PubMed.

A stiffer trunk usually means two practical things: you feel more stable under heavy weight, and many lifters can squat or pull a little more, or get an extra rep or two at the same weight.

What a belt won't do

  • Fix bad technique. If your hips shoot up on deadlifts or you fold forward in the squat, a belt lets you do the same thing with more weight.
  • Replace bracing. A belt only works if you know how to brace. Without that skill, it's an expensive accessory.
  • Guarantee you won't get hurt. The evidence that belts prevent injuries in lifters is thin. Load management, sensible progression and good form do far more.

Who benefits from a belt

A belt makes the most sense if most of these describe you:

  • You've been training consistently for at least 6 to 12 months.
  • Your squat and deadlift are heavy relative to your body weight, and your top sets regularly feel like a grind.
  • You can already brace well without a belt (more on that in our guide on how to brace properly with a belt).
  • Your goal includes moving as much weight as possible on the big barbell lifts.

Who can skip it for now

If you're in your first few months of lifting, a belt adds very little. Your strength is going up every week anyway because you're learning the movements. That window is the best time to build bracing skill with nothing around your waist.

You also don't need one if your training is mostly machines, dumbbells, cables and bodyweight work. Belts earn their keep on heavy compound barbell lifts, not on curls or the leg press.

Signs you're ready for a belt

SignalWhat it looks like
Bracing is automaticYou take a full breath and tighten your trunk on every heavy rep without thinking about it.
Your trunk is the weak linkOn heavy sets, your legs and hips have more to give, but your torso starts to collapse.
Your form holds upYour heavy sets look like your warm-ups, just slower.

Which lifts to wear it on

Squats and deadlifts are the obvious ones. Many lifters also belt up for heavy overhead presses, heavy barbell rows and front squats. There's no need for it on warm-up sets, accessory work, or anything where your trunk isn't under real compressive load.

A simple approach: train your warm-ups and lighter back-off sets beltless, and put the belt on for your heaviest working sets. You keep bracing sharp without the belt and still get its benefit when it matters.

If you do buy one

For most people, a 10mm single-prong or lever belt of uniform 4-inch width is the right first belt. We break down the trade-offs in lever vs prong belts, and FitBesidesHealth has a hands-on roundup of beginner-friendly lifting belts if you want specific models.

Avoid the thin, tapered nylon belts that are wide at the back and narrow at the front. They look supportive, but they give your abs almost nothing to push against, which is the whole point.

Questions lifters ask

Is it bad to lift without a belt?

No. Most lifters train beltless for their first year or more, and plenty of strong people never use one. Lifting beltless builds bracing skill.

Should I wear a belt on every set?

No. Save it for your heaviest working sets, roughly 80% of your max and above. Warm-ups and lighter sets are better done without it.

Does a lifting belt prevent injuries?

There's little evidence that belts prevent injuries on their own. Sensible loading, good technique and recovery matter far more.
