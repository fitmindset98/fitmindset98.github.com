How Tight Should a Lifting Belt Be? The 5-Minute Fit Test

A simple fit test for lifting belts, where the belt should sit for squats and deadlifts, and signs it's too tight or too loose.

By Ryan Kessler. Updated October 10, 2026. 3 min read.

Short answer

After a normal exhale, you should just be able to slide a flat hand between the belt and your stomach. When you take a big breath and brace, your belly should fill that gap and press firmly into the belt all the way around.

Too loose and a belt does nothing. Too tight and you can't get a full breath, which also means it does nothing, except make you miserable. The right fit sits in a narrow window, and finding it takes about five minutes.

The quick fit test

Stand tall, breathe out normally, and put the belt on. Then try to slide your flat hand between the belt and your stomach.

  • Hand slides in with a little resistance: about right.
  • Hand won't go in at all: too tight. You won't be able to expand into the belt.
  • Room for your whole fist: too loose. Your brace has to travel too far before it meets the belt.

Now take a big breath into your belly and brace. Your stomach should fill the gap and press firmly against the leather all the way around. If you feel the pressure only at the front, or not at all at the sides, adjust and try again.

Where the belt should sit

For most people, the belt sits roughly over the belly button, covering the soft area between the bottom of the ribs and the top of the hips. That's the area that expands when you brace.

Small adjustments help depending on the lift:

LiftPositionTightness
SquatCentered over the navel, sometimes slightly higherFirm, one hole tighter than for deadlifts is common
DeadliftOften a little higher, so it doesn't jam into the hips in the start positionSlightly looser, so you can get down to the bar
Overhead pressOver the navelFirm, but you should still be able to breathe between reps

The deadlift adjustment matters more than people expect. If the belt digs into your thighs or ribs when you set up, it can pull you out of a good starting position. If that sounds familiar, check your setup against FitBesidesHealth's step-by-step deadlift form guide before blaming the belt.

Signs it's too tight

  • You can only take a shallow chest breath once it's on.
  • You feel light-headed during the set, beyond the normal effort of a heavy lift.
  • It pinches your ribs or hips enough to change your position.
  • You can't get into the bottom of a squat or down to a deadlift bar the same way you do beltless.

Signs it's too loose

  • The belt slides up or down during the set.
  • You brace hard and feel almost no pressure against it.
  • Heavy sets feel the same with and without it.

Lever belts and the in-between problem

Lever belts are fast to clip on, but the setting is fixed until you unscrew the lever and move it. If your best fit sits between two holes, or you like different tightness for squats and deadlifts, a lever belt can be frustrating. Some lifters keep a spare lever at a second setting. We cover the trade-offs in lever vs prong belts.

Take it off between sets

Don't walk around with a tight belt between sets. Pop the lever or loosen the prong after each heavy set and let your breathing settle. You'll recover better, and you'll get a more consistent fit when you tighten it again.

Fit is only half of it. The other half is the brace itself, which we cover in how to brace properly with a lifting belt.

Questions lifters ask

Should a lifting belt be tighter for squats or deadlifts?

Most lifters wear it slightly tighter for squats. For deadlifts, a notch looser lets you get down to the bar in a good position.

Where should a lifting belt sit?

Roughly over your belly button, covering the area between your lower ribs and the top of your hips. Many lifters wear it a little higher for deadlifts.

Should I keep my belt on between sets?

No. Loosen it after each heavy set so you can breathe and recover, then tighten it again before the next one.
